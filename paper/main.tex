\begin{abstract}
Next-Concept Modeling (NCM) generates text coarse to fine, predicting every position of a
level in parallel, and is reported to be up to 31$\times$ faster than a diffusion language
model. We ask what that speed means on commodity hardware and against the baseline most
practitioners care about: a same-size autoregressive (AR) model with a KV cache. On a
6\,GB RTX~3050 laptop GPU we reproduce the official NCM checkpoints' quality (XSum ROUGE-L
27.8 vs.\ 28.0 reported; Sudoku-hard 84.5\% vs.\ 86.1\%) and benchmark them against
pretrained AR models of similar size under identical conditions, on the full test sets. At
batch size 1, in interleaved timing, NCM ties eager BART on XSum and is 1.26$\times$ slower
than eager opus-mt on WMT14, with a tighter tail on both. At batch size 32 the official
generator is 2.6--9.8$\times$ slower; with a corrected KV cache (upstream fix, verified
here) this shrinks to parity on WMT14 and 2.2$\times$ on XSum at unchanged quality. With both
sides compiled (CUDA graphs), NCM is 1.2--1.7$\times$ faster than AR at batch 1 and
1.7--2.0$\times$ slower at batch 32. On a 4-thread CPU,
cached NCM is 1.1--1.7$\times$ slower than eager AR. With fp16 weights NCM uses
1.3--10$\times$ more GPU memory than the AR models, and its quality is lower
(ROUGE-L 27.9 vs.\ 33.3; BLEU 21.7 vs.\ 33.1). Used as tools around a 3B LLM on all 1,190
XQuAD questions, NCM does not help: compressing retrieved passages cuts RAG accuracy from
73\% to 7\% (BART: 10\%), NCM translation of a German corpus yields 38\% RAG accuracy vs.\
56\% for a small AR translator, and in a tool-calling agent the LLM takes 91--97\% of the
time. A further suite probes where NCM breaks or wins: input and output length, batch
size up to out-of-memory, faithfulness, robustness, out-of-distribution Sudoku, a 3B
general LLM, and agent reliability.
At batch 1, NCM becomes faster than AR only beyond about 30 output tokens, close to the
length of these tasks' outputs; it breaks on inputs longer than a sentence, repeats words
3--7$\times$ more often than the AR baselines, and never flags an unsolvable puzzle.
On unconditional generation up to 1024 tokens (architecture timed with random weights, as the
long checkpoint is unreleased), NCM is 6--16$\times$ faster than compiled GPT-2 models at
batch 1, but the advantage is gone by batch 8 at 512 tokens; at 128 tokens its samples are as
diverse as an in-domain AR model's, but with 1.7$\times$ the perplexity and lower MAUVE (0.80
vs.\ 0.95).
When we train both sides ourselves on identical TinyStories data, a 35M NCM is about
20$\times$ faster than AR models of 1.9--35.9M parameters but scores MAUVE 0.006 against
0.59--0.92, consistently over three seeds at 25M. An official-style level schedule repairs the
finest level but not the coarse ones, and cheaper attention in the generator does not help.
Finally, rank-16 LoRA adapts the released XSum NCM to SAMSum within 0.9 ROUGE-L of full
fine-tuning, though it still trails BART by 14 points.
All code, outputs and per-example logs are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Most of the cost of serving a language model is sequential decoding: an AR model runs one
forward pass per output token. NCM~\citep{ncm2026} instead builds the output in a fixed
number of levels, from a short ``gist'' to the full sequence, predicting all positions of a
level in parallel, in the spirit of next-scale prediction for images~\citep{var2024}. The
headline result is a 31$\times$ speedup, but it is measured against a \emph{diffusion} LM
that needs 128 or more denoising steps, on an H100, with 1024-token outputs.

Practitioners mostly compare against AR models, often on small GPUs, with short outputs,
and increasingly use small models as tools inside retrieval-augmented generation
(RAG)~\citep{rag2020} and agent pipelines. We therefore ask four questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Is NCM faster than a same-size AR model on a laptop GPU, and at what quality?
  \item What does ``31$\times$'' mean in that setting?
  \item Does NCM's speed matter inside RAG and agent pipelines driven by a 3B LLM?
  \item Where are the limits: input and output length, batch size, faithfulness, robustness,
        generalization, and comparison with a general-purpose LLM?
  \item Trained on identical data with the same budget, does NCM beat the AR frontier, and
        how cheaply can a released checkpoint be adapted to a new task?
\end{enumerate}
This is a personal learning project, not a new method: its contribution is an
independent, fully reproducible evaluation on one commodity GPU, with predictions written
down before each benchmark was run and reported as right or wrong.

\section{Background: Next-Concept Modeling}
NCM uses a multiscale VQ-VAE tokenizer that encodes a text sequence into $L$ levels of
discrete codes of increasing length. A transformer generator predicts level $\ell$ in one
pass, conditioned on all coarser levels and an input prefix (the article or source
sentence), so generation takes $L$ passes regardless of output length; the decoder of the
VQ-VAE then maps the finest level back to tokens. We first built a 5.6M-parameter toy
version from scratch to check the mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:toy}), then used the official
task checkpoints for summarization (XSum~\citep{xsum2018}), German$\to$English translation
(WMT14~\citep{wmt2014}) and Sudoku.

\section{Setup}
\paragraph{Hardware and software.} NVIDIA RTX~3050 Laptop GPU (6\,GB), WSL2 Ubuntu 24.04,
PyTorch 2.14 with CUDA 13. All numbers in this paper come from this one machine.

\paragraph{Models.} NCM XSum (88M generator + 60M VQ-VAE) vs.\ BART-base fine-tuned on
XSum~\citep{bart2020} (139M); NCM WMT14 (120M + 144M) vs.\ opus-mt-de-en~\citep{opusmt2020,marian2018}
(74M). The LLM for RAG and agents is qwen2.5:3b~\citep{qwen25}, 4-bit, served by Ollama.

\paragraph{Fairness rules.} Same GPU, precision (fp16 autocast), examples and seed; greedy
decoding for every model; warm-up before timing; \texttt{torch.cuda.synchronize()} around
the clock; only generation is timed; the AR baseline keeps its KV cache; the AR model reads
the same 512-token article prefix as NCM. Quality: ROUGE~\citep{rouge2004} and
sacreBLEU~\citep{bleu2002,sacrebleu2018} on 500 test examples per task.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} For every benchmark we committed predictions to the repository
before running it, and report each as right or wrong.

\section{Toy NCM: the mechanism works, the samples do not}\label{sec:toy}
A 5.6M-parameter NCM (8.6M tokenizer) trained on short stories reconstructs 17.0\% of
tokens from the coarsest level alone and 98.7\% from all six levels, so the coarse-to-fine
representation behaves as intended. Generating 64 tokens it is 9.3$\times$ faster than a
6.9M-parameter AR model with a KV cache at batch 1 and 3.0$\times$ at batch 128 (6 passes vs.\
64). But its samples are far less fluent (GPT-2 perplexity 377 vs.\ 24.5 for AR; real text
13.2): parallel prediction within a level makes local coherence hard.

\section{Reproducing the official checkpoints}
On the first 50 test examples the official evaluation code and our wrapper agree to within
0.01 (XSum ROUGE-L 26.47 vs.\ 26.48; WMT14 BLEU 15.28 for both). On 500 examples we obtain XSum ROUGE-L
27.8 (paper: 28.0 on the full test set) and WMT14 BLEU 20.0 (paper: 22.6). Sudoku solve
rates are 99.0\% (easy), 97.5\% (medium) and 84.5\% (hard) on 200 puzzles each. fp16 is
1.9--2.5$\times$ faster than fp32 with the same quality. The official KV-cache generation path
is 1.5$\times$ faster for WMT14 but drops BLEU from 20.0 to 3.8. Section~\ref{sec:cache}
traces this to a padding bug and measures the fixed cache.

Table~\ref{tab:phase4} and Figure~\ref{fig:phase4} show the central result. At batch 1 all
models run at about 4 examples/s; NCM has a tighter p95 because its number of passes does
not depend on the output. With the official uncached generator, the AR models are

level pass re-runs the transformer over the full input prefix (512 tokens for XSum, 9 times),
whereas BART encodes the article once and runs about 25 cheap cached decoder steps. At
batch 1 the GPU is under-used either way; at batch 32 NCM's repeated prefix work dominates
(7.6\,s of 8.0\,s per batch on XSum). The figure shows the uncached runs.

\paragraph{The KV cache.}\label{sec:cache} The official \texttt{generate(use\_kv\_cache=True)}
skips the prefix padding mask in its cached prefix and level passes, so padded positions
take part in attention (BLEU 3.8). Upstream pull request \#5\footnote{\url{https://github.com/HazyResearch/ncm/pull/5}}
adds the mask, reuses the level-0 pass's prefix keys and values, and enables the cache for
XSum; \#4 fixes a memory leak in \texttt{generate()}. With both, cached outputs are
identical to uncached ones in fp32 (1,024 WMT14 and 256 XSum inputs). In fp16, 94\% (WMT14)
and 97\% (XSum) are identical and quality does not move: BLEU $-0.03$ [$-0.08$, $+0.03$],
ROUGE-L $+0.00$ [$-0.00$, $+0.01$]. Batch-32 throughput rises 2.6$\times$ on WMT14, to
opus-mt's level, and 4.5$\times$ on XSum, which leaves BART 2.2$\times$ ahead. Batch 1 does not
change, because there the GPU is launch-bound, not compute-bound. Most of the batch-32
deficit was therefore missing caching, not the method.

\paragraph{Interleaved timing.} Table~\ref{tab:phase4} comes from single runs on different
days. We re-timed all three models under one protocol: 20 warm-up calls, then 200 rounds in
which each model runs once on the same batch, in rotating order, with
\texttt{cuda.synchronize()} around each call. Ratio intervals are a paired bootstrap over
rounds (Table~\ref{tab:interleaved}). The batch-1 tie holds on XSum but not on WMT14, where
opus-mt is 1.26$\times$ faster; NCM's p95 is lower on both (283 vs.\ 387\,ms, 302 vs.\
344\,ms). With the cache, NCM is 1.8$\times$ faster than opus-mt at batch 8 and level at
batch 32, and 2.0$\times$ slower than BART at batch 32.

\paragraph{Compiled models, fp16 weights, CPU.} Eager Hugging Face \texttt{generate} and
NCM's eager level loop are both launch-bound at batch 1, so the parity above is between two
unoptimized loops. We compile both: the AR models with a static cache and a compiled decoding
step, NCM's two cached passes (prefix and per-level, as factored by the upstream fix) with
CUDA graphs. Quality does not move (AR BLEU 33.06, ROUGE-L 33.25; NCM BLEU $-0.00$
[$-0.07$, $+0.06$], ROUGE-L $+0.00$ [$-0.01$, $+0.02$] vs.\ eager). Compiling speeds AR up
2.1--2.3$\times$ at batch 1 and NCM about 4.6$\times$. Interleaved, compiled NCM is then
faster than compiled AR at batch 1 (0.60 [0.56, 0.64] of opus-mt's median latency, 0.81
[0.76, 0.84] of BART's) and on WMT14 at batch 8 (0.68), with a lower p95; AR is faster at
batch 32 (1.7$\times$ WMT14, 2.0$\times$ XSum) and on XSum from batch 8. One pitfall: NCM
needs a graph per (level, batch shape), and beyond PyTorch's default recompile limit of 8
the remaining levels silently ran eagerly; our harness now aborts instead.
Storing NCM's weights in fp16 (the rows above keep fp32 weights under autocast, while the
AR models load fp16 weights) leaves quality unchanged (BLEU $+0.01$ [$-0.07$, $+0.09$],
ROUGE-L $-0.02$ [$-0.05$, $-0.00$]), cuts peak memory at batch 32 from 3.2 to 2.4\,GB
(WMT14) and 2.2 to 1.3\,GB (XSum), and is about 15\% faster at batch 32 and 3--4\% slower at
batch 1. On a CPU with 4 threads (fp32, batch 1), cached NCM is 1.67$\times$ slower than
eager opus-mt and 1.09$\times$ slower than eager BART: without idle parallel hardware, the
extra positions NCM processes are no longer free.

\section{NCM as a tool in RAG and agents}
\paragraph{Setup.} All 1,190 XQuAD~\citep{xquad2020} questions over its 240 Wikipedia
paragraphs, MiniLM embeddings~\citep{sbert2019} with FAISS~\citep{faiss2019}, top-3
retrieval, qwen2.5:3b answering at temperature 0. We report three scores: lenient
containment (a gold answer string appears in the reply), and SQuAD exact match and token F1.
Rates carry Wilson 95\% intervals. NCM and AR variants are compared on the same questions
with an exact McNemar test (containment, exact match) and a paired bootstrap (F1).
A first run on 40 questions (one per paragraph) overestimated every accuracy, e.g.\ 87.5\%
instead of 73.2\% for plain RAG.

\paragraph{Context compression.} Summarizing each retrieved paragraph before the LLM cuts
containment accuracy from 73.3\% [70.7, 75.7] to 6.6\% [5.3, 8.1] with NCM and 10.2\%
[8.6, 12.0] with BART, although retrieval still finds the right paragraph 96.7\% of the time.
A one-sentence news-style summary keeps the gist and drops the fact the question asks for.
NCM is significantly worse than BART on all three scores (containment $p=2\times10^{-5}$,
exact match $p=0.002$, F1 $-0.044$ [$-0.061$, $-0.028$]). Compression is also slower
(986\,ms with NCM, 714\,ms with BART, vs.\ 417\,ms), because it costs more than the shorter
prompt saves.

\paragraph{Multilingual RAG.} Translating the German version of the corpus (1,265 sentences,
split with a splitter that keeps German ordinals such as ``am 3.\ Oktober'' together) with
NCM gives 37.9\% [35.2, 40.7] containment accuracy vs.\ 55.8\% [53.0, 58.6] with opus-mt;
the English originals give 73.2\%. The gap holds on every score: exact match 28.3\% vs.\
41.2\%, F1 0.465 vs.\ 0.592, all $p<10^{-20}$. Paragraph BLEU against the English originals
is 30.8 vs.\ 46.6. NCM translations replace entity names (``Luthers Schriften'' $\to$
``Marx's writings''), which is fatal for retrieval. Bulk translation ran at 19 vs.\
37 sentences/s at batch 32 with the uncached official code. With the corrected KV cache
(Section~\ref{sec:cache}) NCM's batch-32 throughput on WMT14 matches opus-mt, so the speed
gap is an implementation artefact; the quality gap is not.

\paragraph{Agent.} A hand-written tool-calling loop gives the LLM four tools: search and
three NCM models (summarize, translate, solve Sudoku). Over six multi-step tasks the LLM
accounts for 91--97\% of wall time and NCM tools for 3--9\%, so even an infinitely fast
NCM would barely change end-to-end latency. Prompt design mattered more than speed: with a
first prompt the LLM spent 28\,s and 1,449 tokens solving the Sudoku itself instead of calling the tool.

\section{Limits suite}\label{sec:limits}
Each experiment below pushes one axis until something breaks. Predictions are in
\texttt{docs/limits.md}, committed before running.

\subsection{Input length}
Inputs are $k$ consecutive WMT14 test sentences joined together, so length grows in a
controlled way and the reference stays exact; the AR model is allowed up to 400 output
tokens so its default cap is not what is measured.
\limitstable{input_length}{\texttt{python -m ncm\_lab.limits.run input-length}}
\limitsfig{input_length}{BLEU vs.\ input length on WMT14 De$\to$En.}{figure from
\texttt{input-length}}
NCM degrades as soon as inputs approach its prefix window: BLEU falls from 16.3 to 10.1
with two sentences and to 2.6 with three, when 15\% and then 54\% of inputs are truncated,
and its outputs shrink to 37\% of the reference length. opus-mt shows no loss over the whole
range. The checkpoint is a sentence-level translator; longer inputs need chunking.

\subsection{Batch size up to out-of-memory}
\limitstable{batch_sweep}{\texttt{python -m ncm\_lab.limits.run batch-sweep}}
\limitsfig{batch_sweep}{Throughput vs.\ batch size; markers end where the model runs out of
6\,GB.}{figure from \texttt{batch-sweep}}
The uncached generator's throughput saturates early: on XSum BART is faster from batch 2,
and on WMT14 NCM leads only at batch 2 and 4 and is overtaken from 16. No configuration
raised an out-of-memory error. At batch 128 NCM's peak allocation (7.8 and 9.4\,GB) exceeds
the 6\,GB card and the driver spills into shared system memory, which makes each XSum example
4.8$\times$ slower than at batch 64. This suite ran before the cache fix and with fp32
weights; Table~\ref{tab:phase4} has the cached numbers.

\subsection{Output length: where NCM's fixed step count pays off}
The AR model is forced to write exactly $L$ tokens and its latency is fitted as
$a + bL$; NCM's cost is constant up to its canvas size. The break-even $L^\ast = (t_\text{NCM}
- a)/b$ is NCM's speed niche on this GPU. This measures speed only: forced AR outputs are not
meaningful text, and NCM cannot write beyond its canvas.
\limitstable{output_length_breakeven}{\texttt{python -m ncm\_lab.limits.run output-length}}
\limitsfig{output_length}{Latency at batch 1 vs.\ output length.}{figure from
\texttt{output-length}}
At batch 1 the AR baseline becomes slower than NCM beyond about 30 output tokens (27 on
XSum, 31 on WMT14). Both canvases (48 and 64 tokens) are longer than that, but typical
outputs here are about 20 words, just below the break-even, which is why the two tie at
batch 1. At batch 8 the break-even rises to 39 (WMT14) and 120 tokens (XSum, beyond the
canvas). This is the setting where NCM's design should pay off, and the released task
checkpoints barely reach it.

\subsection{Faithfulness}
Abstractive summarizers are known to hallucinate~\citep{maynez2020}. We count outputs that
contain a number or a capitalized name absent from the source (for translation: absent from
source and reference), and outputs with a repeated word or bigram. These proxies over-count
equally for every model, so we compare models rather than read absolute rates.
\limitstable{faithfulness}{\texttt{python -m ncm\_lab.limits.run faithfulness} (CPU only)}
NCM's outputs contain an invented number about twice as often as BART's on XSum (20\%
vs.\ 11\%), and three times as often an invented name in translation (21\% vs.\ 7\%). The
largest gap is repetition: 38\% vs.\ 5\% of summaries and 36\% vs.\ 13\% of translations,
despite the official frequency and adjacency penalties on XSum.

\subsection{Robustness to noisy input}
\limitstable{robustness}{\texttt{python -m ncm\_lab.limits.run robustness}}
Contrary to our prediction, NCM is not markedly more fragile in absolute terms: typos,
lowercasing and missing punctuation cost both models a similar number of points on WMT14.
Relative to its lower clean score NCM loses more, and on XSum it is clearly hurt more by
missing punctuation ($-6.3$ vs.\ $-3.4$ ROUGE-L) and by uppercase input (it keeps 19\% of
its ROUGE-L, BART 40\%). Uppercase input breaks both.

\subsection{Sudoku out of distribution}
NCM vs.\ an exact backtracking solver and qwen2.5:3b on: the hard test set; the same puzzles
with digits relabelled and the grid transposed (logically identical); 5 or 15 clues removed
(any valid completion counts); unsolvable puzzles (a duplicate digit in a row, where the
right answer is to say so); and already-solved grids.
\limitstable{sudoku_stress}{\texttt{python -m ncm\_lab.limits.run sudoku-stress}}
NCM keeps its accuracy on relabelled and transposed puzzles (85\% vs.\ 84\%), so it has
not memorised the test grids. It does not generalise to sparser puzzles (32\% with five
clues removed, 1\% with fifteen), and it always returns a full grid: it never flags an
unsolvable puzzle, and it fails to return any already-solved grid unchanged. An exact
backtracking solver gets 99--100\% of every set right, in under 3\,ms per puzzle except on
the sparsest set (183\,ms), and qwen2.5:3b solves only already-solved grids.

\subsection{A specialist vs.\ a general LLM}
The same 100 examples per task through NCM, the AR baseline and zero-shot qwen2.5:3b, one
request at a time, end-to-end latency.
\limitstable{llm_vs_ncm}{\texttt{python -m ncm\_lab.limits.run llm-vs-ncm}}
\limitsfig{llm_vs_ncm}{Quality vs.\ median latency at batch 1.}{figure from
\texttt{llm-vs-ncm}}
The specialist is 3.5$\times$ (XSum) and 2.0$\times$ (WMT14) faster per request than a
3B general model, and writes better XSum-style summaries (ROUGE-L 27.6 vs.\ 19.7), but the
LLM translates better (BLEU 21.9 vs.\ 16.2). The pretrained AR baselines beat both on
quality at similar latency to NCM. The LLM runs in a different runtime (Ollama), so its
latencies are indicative only.

\subsection{Agent reliability}
Each Phase 5 task runs 10 times at temperature 0.7 (and once at 0).
\limitstable{agent_reliability}{\texttt{python -m ncm\_lab.limits.run agent-reliability}}
Sampling barely matters: whether the LLM picks the right tools is fixed per task, not
random. Four tasks succeed in every run; on two the LLM skips a needed tool in every run, at
temperature 0 and 0.7 alike. On Sudoku it calls the right tool but passes a malformed
puzzle in 10 of 11 runs and then gives up. Reliability here is a prompt and argument-format
problem, not a property of the tool.

\section{Long unconditional outputs}\label{sec:long}
The paper times 1024-token unconditional generation, but the 1024-token OpenWebText
checkpoint is not released; every released unconditional checkpoint has a 128-token canvas.
We therefore measure quality at 128 tokens with the real TinyStories NCM (125M), and speed
from 128 to 1024 tokens with upstream's OpenWebText architecture and \emph{random weights}
(317--390M; latency depends on shapes and pass count, not on the weights). Every model samples
with temperature 1 and no truncation, as upstream's evaluation does, and runs in bf16 (fp16

\section{A controlled comparison: NCM vs.\ an AR ladder}\label{sec:scale}
Every comparison above sets an NCM trained by its authors against AR models trained by
others, on other data. To isolate the method, we trained everything ourselves on the same
data: all of TinyStories cut to 256 tokens (2.1M stories, 424M real tokens), the same 4096-token
BPE, the same optimizer, and 200M training positions per model. The NCM is a 10.7M multiscale
residual-VQ tokenizer with 9 levels (1, 2, 4, \dots, 256 codes; 96.6\% token reconstruction)
and a 12.5M or 24.6M block-causal generator; the AR ladder spans 1.9M to 35.9M parameters,
the largest matching the NCM's total footprint (35.3M). Each model samples at the temperature
(0.6--1.0) with the best MAUVE on 256 validation samples, then writes 512 test samples, scored
like Table~\ref{tab:long-quality}. Latency is the median of 20 batch-1 samples on the laptop
GPU. Pre-registered stop rule: if the 25M NCM with its best decoding stays above 3$\times$ the
perplexity of the AR model that matches its latency, stop scaling.

NCM is about 20$\times$ faster per sample and far worse (Table~\ref{tab:scale},
Figure~\ref{fig:scale}): even the 1.9M AR model beats the 35M NCM by two orders of magnitude on
MAUVE. Doubling the generator barely helps (MAUVE 0.005 $\to$ 0.006), and the samples open
fluently and then stutter (``her her her mom her'', ``to to''). The speed gap is an upper bound:
the AR time is dominated by per-token Python and kernel-launch overhead (the 1.9M model is
barely faster than the 6.9M one), which a fused or CUDA-graph decoder would cut several-fold.

\paragraph{Where the quality is lost.} We encoded real test stories, kept their first $k$
levels and let the generator sample the rest. The tokenizer alone (all 9 levels real)
reaches MAUVE 0.863 and perplexity 12.9, close to the AR models. Generating only the last
level, with every coarser level perfect, drops MAUVE to 0.010 (0.012 greedy, perplexity 61);
levels 1--6 add almost nothing on top (perplexity 181 $\to$ 212). The last level's 256 codes
are predicted in one pass without seeing each other, so greedy decoding with perfect context
is the best any sampling rule can do, and it is still 4$\times$ the perplexity of the 1.9M AR
model, which is 11$\times$ slower and the closest in latency. The stop rule is met.

\paragraph{Robustness.} The gap does not depend on the seed: over three training seeds the
26.4M AR model scores MAUVE 0.918 $\pm$ 0.027 and the 24.6M NCM 0.0059 $\pm$ 0.0002. Nor does
it depend on decoding. We tried top-$k$ 5 and 20, a temperature that falls from 1.0 to 0.5
across levels, greedy decoding on the last one or two levels, and greedy decoding everywhere;
every setting stays between 0.004 and 0.007. As a second opinion we asked qwen2.5:3b to rate
coherence from 1 to 5, blind, on 200 stories per model. It passes its control (human stories
4.08, the same stories with shuffled sentences 3.17) and agrees with MAUVE on the main point:
the AR models get 2.54--2.82, the NCMs 1.37--1.62, and not one NCM story is rated 4 or 5. It is
too coarse, however, to rank the AR models by size.

\paragraph{Is this a weak reimplementation?} A result this poor deserves suspicion, since the
released 125M TinyStories NCM reaches MAUVE 0.80 (Section~\ref{sec:long}). The most visible
difference from the official design is our level schedule: it doubles at every level, which
leaves half the positions to the last one. We therefore trained a second tokenizer with an
official-style schedule scaled to 256 positions (12 levels: 1, 16, 32, \dots, 216, 256; 4096
codes; 12.1M parameters), and a 22.4M generator whose output heads are tied to the codebooks,
both on 100M positions. The new tokenizer's reconstructions are as good as human text (MAUVE
0.953), and given the coarser levels the generator now writes the last one almost perfectly
(0.939, against 0.010 with the original tokenizer). Free samples, however, still score 0.007.
Quality drops to 0.57 when the generator has to write the last three levels and to 0.04 when it
writes five. So the finest-level failure was partly an artefact of our schedule, but the
generator's inability to write the coarse levels is not. At 25M parameters and 100--200M
positions it is the method, not our level schedule, that loses to AR. Whether it catches up at
the scale of the released model remains open.

\section{Does the generator need full attention?}\label{sec:mixers}
Keeping the original tokenizer and a 100M-position budget, we swapped the generator's
attention for three cheaper alternatives: attention within each level only (no cross-level
attention), depthwise convolutions on the levels of 64 positions or more, and block-causal
linear attention. All four generators end up on the same MAUVE floor (0.005--0.007), so MAUVE
cannot separate them; teacher-forced loss at the finest level can. Full attention is best at
2.51 nats, followed by per-level attention (2.56), linear attention (2.76) and convolution
(2.77). Only convolution is faster, and mainly on the CPU: 1.5$\times$ at 1024 positions and
2.0$\times$ at 2048, against 1.14$\times$ on the GPU at 2048. At this scale, attention is not
what holds the NCM back.

\section{Adapting a released NCM: SAMSum}\label{sec:finetune}
The official repository ships inference code only, so we first rebuilt the XSum NCM's
training loss. Because each level attends only to the prompt and to itself, a single
teacher-forced pass yields every level's logits, and its argmax matches greedy generation on
100\% of codes. We then fine-tuned the released checkpoint and \texttt{bart-base-xsum} on SAMSum
dialogue summaries for 3 epochs, with 3 seeds per arm, and scored ROUGE-L on all 819 test
dialogues (Table~\ref{tab:samsum}). The frozen VQ tokenizer already rebuilds 98.9\% of summary
tokens, so we left it frozen.

\section{Discussion}
On a laptop GPU with short outputs, NCM's parallel decoding buys predictable single-request
latency. Throughput comes only once the input prefix is cached: without the cache, every
pass repeats prefix work that an AR model does once. With the corrected cache it matches a
pretrained AR translator at batch 32, but it still trails on summarization (512-token
inputs) and on quality. On long unconditional outputs (Section~\ref{sec:long}) the
single-request advantage is large, 6--16$\times$ against compiled AR models at batch 1 up to
1024 tokens, but it comes from the fixed pass count rather than from length, and it is gone by
batch 8 at 512 tokens. We did not compare against diffusion models, the paper's baseline. As a tool in LLM pipelines, NCM's speed is dwarfed by the LLM, and
its weaknesses (lower quality than pretrained baselines, invented entities) are exactly the
ones RAG punishes.
The limits suite locates the niche: on this GPU, NCM's batch-1 advantage starts at about 30
output tokens, which the task outputs here barely reach. Its main failure modes are inputs
beyond its prefix window, repetition, and a fixed output format that cannot express ``no
answer''.

\section{Limitations}
One GPU and one machine; results on an H100 or with larger batches may differ. Baselines are
pretrained while NCM checkpoints are not, so the quality gap is not only the method's.
Phase 4 and RAG use full test sets, but the agent has only six tasks, and the limits suite
uses smaller samples. Only the ratios in Table~\ref{tab:interleaved} are interleaved and
have intervals; the GPU ran at 66--88\,$^\circ$C with the SM clock between 1.2 and 1.9\,GHz,
so absolute times vary between runs. Except where marked as fp16 weights, NCM runs fp16 autocast over fp32 weights. Compiled rows compile only NCM's generator passes, not its tokenizer decoder or penalty loop. We did not re-measure the diffusion baseline behind the
31$\times$ claim. Long-output timings above 128 tokens use random weights, and long-output
quality is measured only at 128 tokens; the long runs use bf16 throughout. Faithfulness uses string proxies, not human judgment. The validity-check and attention-variant generators have one seed at half the main budget, and the validity check changes the schedule and the tied heads together.
